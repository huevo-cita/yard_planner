# Crop Guide — families, spacing, and what each crop actually needs

Spacing is square-foot-gardening style: plants per 1 ft square. Pest notes are Central Texas
specific. Family drives rotation.

## Rotation families

Nightshade, cucurbit, brassica, legume, allium, umbellifer, chenopod. Anything in the same
family shares pests and soil diseases, so those are the groupings rotation cares about.

Note the trap: **radish, arugula and turnip are brassicas.** People rotate broccoli away from a
square and then plant radishes in it. Same family, same problem.

Legumes (beans, southern peas) fix nitrogen and are the natural follow-on to heavy feeders.

## The management each crop actually needs

**Tomatoes.** Cage or stake at transplant, never later. For indeterminate varieties, pinch out
suckers — the shoots in the crotch between the main stem and a branch — up to the first flower
cluster, which puts energy into fruit instead of foliage. Water evenly; irregular watering
causes blossom-end rot far more often than a calcium deficiency does. Once nights hold above
75°F, fruit set stops no matter what you do; that's the signal to plan the fall crop, not to
troubleshoot.

**Peppers.** Slower and less dramatic than tomatoes. Pinch the first flower or two off young
plants so they build a frame first. They keep producing into fall once established.

**Cucumbers.** Trellis them, always. Vertical growing keeps the fruit straight and clean, keeps
foliage dry against powdery mildew, and returns most of the bed. Pick every couple of days;
one cucumber left to yellow tells the plant it's done.

**Okra.** Harvest every 2–3 days without exception. Pods go from tender to woody in about 48
hours in Austin heat, and a plant left with mature pods stops producing. Pick at 3–4 inches.
Wear sleeves, the leaves irritate skin. Plants reach 5–6 feet, so give them the north end.

**Southern peas.** Direct sow, then largely ignore. Aphids get knocked off with a hose. Harvest
green for fresh shelling, or let the pods dry on the plant for dried peas — dried needs a May
or June sowing to finish before frost.

**Sweet potatoes.** Plant slips, not tubers. Give them a grow bag or a corner where the vines
can run 10 feet. Harvest before frost, then cure at warm room temperature for 1–2 weeks; skipping
the cure is why home-grown sweet potatoes taste bland and don't keep.

**Melons.** Small varieties only in a raised bed, trellised, with a fabric sling under each
fruit as it sizes up. If bees are scarce, hand-pollinate in the morning: pick a male flower
(plain stem), strip the petals, and dab pollen onto the female flower (tiny fruit behind the
bloom). Harvest when the stem cracks around the fruit on its own.

**Beans.** Direct sow, never transplant. Bush beans give one concentrated flush, so succession
sow every 2–3 weeks. Pole beans on a trellis produce longer from less ground.

**Basil.** Pinch the growing tips constantly and remove flower spikes the moment they appear.
Flowering ends leaf production and turns the flavor bitter.

**Brassicas (broccoli, kale, cabbage).** Floating row cover goes on at transplant, before
cabbage moths ever arrive — this prevents the entire looper problem rather than treating it.
Harvest kale and collards by taking the outer 2–3 leaves per plant; never cut the growing point.

**Carrots.** Thinning is not optional. Sow thickly, then thin with scissors — snip at soil
level rather than pulling, which disturbs the neighbors. Unthinned carrots produce nothing.

**Lettuce.** Cut-and-come-again: scissor the outer leaves an inch above the crown and it
regrows for weeks. Succession sow every 2 weeks for a continuous supply.

**Garlic.** Plant cloves pointy-end-up, 2 inches deep, 4 inches apart in mid-October; harvest
in May when the lower third of the leaves brown. It holds its square for seven months.

## Pests, in escalation order

| Pest | Crops | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Everything, new growth | Hard water blast every 3 days; squish; insecticidal soap at dusk on one plant as a last resort |
| Cabbage loopers / worms | Brassicas | Row cover from day one prevents it entirely; hand-pick into soapy water |
| Harlequin bugs | Brassicas | Hand-pick (they're slow); crush the barrel-striped eggs on leaf undersides |
| Squash vine borer | Squash, zucchini | Wrap the base of the stem in foil at planting; the only reliable prevention |
| Leaf-footed / stink bugs | Tomatoes, chard | Hand-pick in cool mornings while they're sluggish |
| Spider mites | Anything heat-stressed | Water blasts on leaf undersides; they hate humidity |
| Slugs and snails | Lettuce, seedlings | Evening flashlight patrol; Sluggo (iron phosphate) is safe for frogs, cats and kids |
| Fire ants | Anywhere | 2–3 gallons of boiling water on the mound, early morning |
| Nematodes | Nightshades, repeated in one spot | No treatment — this is what rotation prevents |

**Do not use** Bt, neem, spinosad, or pyrethrins in a garden with butterflies. Bt kills every
caterpillar, including the black swallowtails the parsley and dill are there to feed. Frogs
absorb chemicals through their skin, so nothing goes near a water feature.

**The good guys.** Black swallowtail caterpillars (green, black stripes, yellow dots) on
parsley and dill are the point — move them, never remove them. Ladybug and lacewing larvae look
alarming and eat aphids by the hundred.
